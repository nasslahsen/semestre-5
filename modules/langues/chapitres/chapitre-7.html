The text

When employees give something extra

A text about three American companies whose employees thank their bosses, with vocabulary, a true or false and three questions (6 and 8 points on the sheet). The sheet has no answer key: the answers on this site are suggested.

The plan of the text

A question (what makes employees happy and productive?), three examples, Piggly-Wiggly Carolina (paragraphs 2 and 3), Delta Airlines (4 and 5), Control Data (6), and a conclusion (7): committed employees make a company stable and productive.

The text

Paragraph 1. What makes employees happy and productive at work? This question is asked everywhere in American big business. Companies look at Japanese productivity with envy and want to know how to get their own workers to feel a part of the company and to want to produce. When a business runs into financial trouble, the work force may help the company pull through the crisis if the workers feel responsible and important.

Paragraph 2. When Piggly-Wiggly Carolina, a South Carolina grocery store and distributing business, was approached by a large corporation with a buy-out offer, they considered it seriously. There had been a number of local businesses which had closed; many people were jobless. But even though they were feeling financial pressure, the owners of Piggly-Wiggly turned down the buy-out offer.

Paragraph 3. The relieved employees organized a campaign to buy a tractor trailer and give it to the company. They raised $40,000 in the “Rig for the Pig” drive. Then they threw a big party for their bosses and unveiled the big truck. Everyone sang the company's commercial song; truckers in overalls, executives in coat and tie, secretaries, they all sang together. And everyone wore the big button that announced “I'm Piggly-Wiggly Proud!” Hundreds of balloons saying “Thanks, Piggly-Wiggly” went sailing up to the sky. This was the way the employees showed their appreciation to the company. They had seen their friends lose their jobs in the community, but they themselves were safe in their own jobs. There had never even been a layoff in the two generations that had run the business. They felt like a 10-year mechanic, Marion Smith, who said, “When you can work for a company as good to you as this one, you just feel like doing good for them.”

Paragraph 4. The idea of giving something to their company had been borrowed from the people who worked at Delta Airlines and were proud of their company. For the first time in decades, the quarterly report from Delta had shown a loss. But Delta had gone ahead and given an 8 percent raise to its 36,000 employees anyway. The people who worked at Delta felt they had a lot to be happy about. The airline avoids laying workers off by shifting them to other jobs, so stewards sometimes wind up as clerks, and pilots may be moving luggage around, but they still have a job. And maybe the most important thing is that the management really seems to listen to employee problems. Every year employees meet with upper managers, without their direct supervisors, to air their grievances and get the difficulties settled.

Paragraph 5. And the company has an “open-door” policy so that it is easy for workers to speak to management. When several stewardesses thought up the idea of giving a plane to the company, they had no trouble getting to talk to the vice-president for finance right away. Three-quarters of the workers contributed to buy the plane, and the day “The Spirit of Delta” was given to Delta it went right into service.

Paragraph 6. One of America's biggest computer companies, Control Data, has over 50,000 employees spread out across the world. But it makes special efforts to keep the headquarters in Minneapolis, Minnesota, in touch with the work force. It has developed “involvement teams” which are supposed to be like Japan's “quality circles,” helping workers to solve the work-related problems by themselves. Control Data has gone farther than many other companies in employee-satisfaction programs such as a psychological and economic counseling service, a day-care center, physical fitness classes, flexible work schedules, and a special questionnaire to help spot problem areas at work.

Paragraph 7. The new American business style recognizes that unless workers feel committed to their company they will not be a stable work force, and they will not be as productive as they could be. Those businesses which make an effort to keep their employees happy will be the businesses that succeed in the marketplace.

Three traps in the true or false

  • the passive: Piggly-Wiggly was approached by a large corporation (the other company made the offer);
  • who wore what: truckers in overalls, executives in coat and tie;
  • what was borrowed: the idea, not a truck.

When employees give something extra: answers

Suggested answers: the sheet has no key. Tap a dashed box to see its answer.

I. Vocabulary

  1. succeed in avoiding difficulty (1)
  2. refused (2)

  8. find; locate (6)

II. True or false (6 points)

  1. Some American companies think that the productivity of Japanese workers is better than that of their workers.
  2. Piggly-Wiggly Carolina approached a large corporation with a buy-out offer.
  3. The employees were relieved because the owners of Piggly-Wiggly turned down the buy-out offer.
  4. Executives wore overalls at the party.
  5. Piggly-Wiggly employees borrowed a truck from the people who worked at Delta Airlines.
  6. The workers at Delta were happy because they had been given an 8 % raise even though Delta was losing money.

III. Answer the questions (8 points)

  1. What are the two benefits the new American business would get if its workers feel committed to their jobs?
  2. Explain what is meant by “involvement teams”.
  3. In your country, what is the reaction of employees if their company is in trouble?
